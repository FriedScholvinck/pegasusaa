What should the expectations from Single European Sky really be?

13 January 2023 · Graham Lake

Former director general of air traffic management trade body CANSO, Graham Lake, argues the much-criticised Single European Sky project deserves credit for what has been achieved, but that better expectations management and fuller accountability for lack of progress and investment decisions at a local level is required.

Criticism of the Single European Sky is growing. IATA Director General Willie Walsh recently described it as “a scandal”, while Ryanair group chief executive Michael O’Leary asserted that SES is a failure. Both called for political action and highlighted how the more efficient routings intended by SES will deliver additional carbon savings of 10–12%.

Meanwhile, the Commission wrestles with the challenge of what to do about SES. The politics of sovereignty, defence and workers’ rights present formidable obstructions to further progress. While air navigation service providers point to significant individual green achievements, the uncomfortable truth exercising airline chiefs is fragmented European airspace.

Is SES criticism fair?

Criticism is not new. The European Commission’s own audit acknowledged that SES had succeeded in changing culture, but not in delivering a Single European Sky.

Using 2005 as a base, the original goals were to triple capacity, reduce environmental effects by 10%, reduce ATM costs to airspace users by 50%, and increase safety tenfold. When demand growth slowed, the capacity goals became less relevant and cost-efficiency goals unachievable. By 2018, carbon targets had acquired more focus.

SES created a culture of convergence among European states and regulators and led to the pan-European Performance Review Body. SESAR has supported harmonisation, while Eurocontrol as Network Manager has accelerated convergence and clarified accountabilities. These successes should be lauded, yet the objectives have not been achieved as intended and Covid further disrupted budgets, programmes and manpower planning.

What can be done?

Each stakeholder needs a deeper understanding of the performance and cost elements they control or influence. Airlines and airports could better define their operational, environmental and financial priorities for national ATM systems and agree delivery locally and nationally. Reliance on the nirvana of SES to solve systemic challenges will continue to disappoint.

Airports also need greater clarity and resilience in capacity planning. An airport unable to support planned operation 24/7 creates risk for aircraft and airspace operators.

Airline criticism is understandable, but beneficial progress has been made. SES deserves credit for significant achievements. Better expectations management and fuller local accountability are nevertheless in order. Stakeholders deserve clarity on how investments will pay back in safety, efficiency, environmental and cost performance.

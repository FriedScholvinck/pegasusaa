How can we know when options are exhausted amid the noise over Schiphol?

8 May 2023 · Graham Lake

Reducing noise impacts

On 24 June 2022 the Dutch government announced that flights from Amsterdam’s Schiphol Airport would be limited to 440,000 a year, 11% fewer than in 2019, effective from November 2023. The stated objective was to cut noise pollution and restore the balance between a well-operating international airport, the business climate and a healthier living environment.

Aircraft noise increasingly concerns communities near airports or under flight paths. Reflecting ICAO’s Balanced Approach, EU regulation 598 sets out the process to follow when considering restrictions for noise reasons.

KLM, Delta, Corendon, easyJet and TUI challenged the decision, arguing that noise and CO2 could be reduced while maintaining networks through investment in newer, quieter and more fuel-efficient aircraft. In April 2023, a Dutch court blocked the cut because the authorities had not followed the correct procedure.

The court noted the need to exhaust other mitigation methods before restricting flying. Aircraft operators, airports, air navigation service providers and local planning authorities should explore options, evaluate economic and health factors and consult stakeholders. Only then can governments or airports reasonably resort to flight restrictions.

The approach balances aviation and economic needs with local communities. Noise-abatement procedures, quieter aircraft, land-use planning and insulation can minimise restrictions.

Although the ruling was a near-term victory for airlines, the Dutch government continued consulting on a permanent restriction. Schiphol separately announced plans to ban private jets, restrict operating times, abandon a second runway and prohibit the noisiest aircraft. Moving aircraft elsewhere, however, relocates rather than reduces noise.

The net-zero link

Measures that improve noise performance are often linked to efficient flight profiles. Continuous climb and descent trajectories reduce both noise and emissions, while the drive to net zero will reduce levels of aircraft noise reaching the ground.

The industry’s ability to control noise impact has limits, but lessons learned by operators, ANSPs, regulators and planners can inform others. Even at Schiphol, more can be done through collaboration and transparency.

The challenge is determining fairly that every other measure has been exhausted. Airspace modernisation is inevitably long term and requires government support, yet will yield real benefits. Exhaustion should be independently evidenced and verified. The industry at Schiphol and elsewhere should seek compelling, evidenced answers from governments and stakeholders.
